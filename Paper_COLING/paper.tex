\title{What Did the Prompt Edit Break? Item-Level Regression Tracking\texorpdfstring{\\}{ }for Automatic Prompt Optimization}

\begin{abstract}
Prompts in production LLM pipelines are revised repeatedly, and a revision that fixes some inputs can silently break others. Automatic prompt optimization (APO) automates such revisions but is usually judged by overall scores, which do not reveal which answers become correct, which correct answers become wrong, or whether gains extend to unseen questions. Motivated by this problem in an industrial document-processing pipeline, we ask whether repeatedly feeding back the observed effect of each revision can reduce regressions while keeping accuracy gains. We introduce Reflective Feedback-Driven Prompt Optimization (RFDPO), which tracks recoveries (incorrect to correct), regressions (correct to incorrect), and churn (all changed outcomes), returns them to the optimizer, and records every prompt change, so each optimizer action can be traced to its effect. We evaluate RFDPO on five benchmarks with GPT-5 as optimizer and GPT-4o-mini and GPT-4.1 Mini as solvers. In five of nine configurations, churn was at least five times the absolute net change, and a single-item net change concealed up to 17 changed outcomes. Average validation gains were 7.9 points, against 5.2 on held-out tests. In an ablation, item-level feedback reduced churn and regressions at similar net gains.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Prompts in deployed LLM applications are maintained like code: they are revised when a new input type fails, a model version changes, or an output format must be tightened. In machine-learning systems such changes rarely stay local~\citep{sculley2015debt}; engineers who build LLM products describe prompt revision as trial and error and name testing as a main difficulty~\citep{parnin2023copilot}, most prompt changes in open-source repositories are not documented in commit messages~\citep{tafreshipour2025prompting}, and hosted models change behavior across versions and repeated runs~\citep{chen2023drift,atil2025nondeterminism}.

This work began with that problem in an industrial document-processing pipeline, where LLM prompts extract fields from scanned documents. Revisions that fixed one class of documents could change answers, or break the required output format~\citep{liu2024structured}, on documents that had been processed correctly, and an aggregate score did not show which. Automatic prompt optimization (APO), in which an optimizer LLM revises a solver's prompt from evaluation feedback~\citep{zhou2023ape,yang2024opro,pryzant2023protegi}, shares this blind spot: methods are compared by accuracy before and after optimization. For hosted-model updates, \citet{ma2024regression} found that previously correct predictions broke in 87.9\% of cases where aggregate accuracy improved.

Consider two prompt revisions that each improve accuracy by two examples: the first corrects two examples without new failures, while the second corrects ten but breaks eight. Aggregate accuracy cannot distinguish them or show which changes should inform subsequent revisions. Gains on tuning examples may also fail to transfer to held-out data~\citep{recht2019imagenet}, especially when validation examples serve as optimization feedback.

These observations raise a practical question: if the optimizer is repeatedly shown which examples its previous revision fixed and which it broke, can it reduce regressions while keeping its accuracy gains? To study it, we introduce \emph{Reflective Feedback-Driven Prompt Optimization} (RFDPO; Figure~\ref{fig:arch}), which identifies \emph{recoveries} (incorrect to correct), \emph{regressions} (correct to incorrect), and \emph{churn} (all changed outcomes) and, from the second round, returns them to the optimizer with the prompt sections it modified. Unlike approaches that guide revisions with aggregate scores, current errors, or execution traces, RFDPO feeds back the observed consequences of the preceding edit. Because RFDPO records every prompt version, outcome, and cost, it acts as a scanner for prompt optimization: each optimizer action can be traced to its effect on individual examples. \textcolor{red}{RFDPO is not yet deployed; because the pipeline's documents cannot be released, we evaluate it on public benchmarks.}

On five benchmarks with GPT-4o-mini and GPT-4.1 Mini as solvers, churn was at least five times the absolute net change in five of nine configurations, and a single-item net change concealed up to 17 changed outcomes. Average test gains were about 66\% of validation gains. In an ablation, item-level feedback was associated with about 40\% lower mining-set churn and fewer regressions at similar net gains. Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\setlength{\topsep}{1pt}

\item \textbf{An auditable prompt optimization tool} that feeds item-level recoveries and regressions back into prompt revision and records every revision with its effect (\S\ref{sec:method}).

\item \textbf{An empirical characterization of prompt revisions}, showing how aggregate accuracy conceals changes in individual outcomes and how validation gains differ from test gains (\S\ref{sec:results}).

\item \textbf{An analysis of feedback and editing behavior}, including an ablation in which item-level feedback reduces churn and regressions at similar net gains (\S\ref{sec:edits}, \S\ref{sec:discussion}).

\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Automatic prompt optimization.}
APO has been explored through discrete token search~\citep{shin2020autoprompt}, instruction generation and selection~\citep{zhou2023ape}, LLM-based optimization~\citep{yang2024opro}, textual gradients~\citep{pryzant2023protegi,yuksekgonul2025textgrad}, evolutionary approaches~\citep{guo2024evoprompt,fernando2024promptbreeder}, and the optimization of multi-stage pipelines or structured prompts~\citep{opsahlong2024miprov2,schnabel2024sammo,khattab2024dspy}. MPO~\citep{sharma2026mpo} uses the same five-section prompt structure as RFDPO. GEPA~\citep{agrawal2026gepa} uses reflective prompt mutation and Pareto-based selection, Trace2Policy~\citep{zha2026trace2policy} applies a regression gate when refining decision rules, and self-feedback methods revise through verbal critiques~\citep{madaan2023selfrefine,shinn2023reflexion}. RFDPO instead returns the observed item-level consequences of the preceding edit to the optimizer, and it retains changed candidates even when validation performance decreases while recording their effects.

\paragraph{Behavioral evaluation and regression tracking.}
Aggregate accuracy can conceal important changes in model behavior~\citep{ribeiro2020checklist}; negative flips have motivated regression-free model updating~\citep{yan2021positive}, and paired outcome comparisons have a long history in statistical evaluation~\citep{mcnemar1947sampling}. For LLM applications, previously correct behavior can break following API updates~\citep{ma2024regression}, and small prompt changes can alter predictions~\citep{salinas2024butterfly,sclar2024quantifying,errica2025wrong}, although some apparent sensitivity results from evaluation artifacts~\citep{hua2025flaw}. Our work tracks labeled recoveries and regressions across successive optimization revisions and measures churn to distinguish the extent of behavioral change from the net accuracy difference. This distinction matters most when recovered examples are offset by regressions, leaving aggregate performance nearly unchanged.

\paragraph{Evaluation protocols and generalization.}
Published APO scores depend on data splits, solver models, baseline prompts, and feedback availability, and are not directly comparable across protocols (Appendix~\ref{app:prior}). Improvements on tuning data may not generalize~\citep{recht2019imagenet}, and contamination presents additional concerns~\citep{oren2023proving}. In our diagnostic experiment, providing test examples as feedback yielded an accuracy of 95.3\%, compared with 73.4\% when they were withheld (Appendix~\ref{app:oracle}). RFDPO therefore excludes test examples from feedback and selection, and we treat validation performance, which also feeds the optimizer, as a tuning measure.

\section{RFDPO}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Prompt Representation and Evaluation}

RFDPO uses a solver LLM to execute tasks and an optimizer LLM to revise prompts, without modifying the solver's parameters (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}). Following MPO~\citep{sharma2026mpo}, each prompt contains five fixed sections (System Role, Context, Task Details, Constraints, Output Format), allowing RFDPO to track which parts of a prompt are modified. Mining examples ($M$) provide feedback for revisions; validation examples ($V$) are used for selection and, from the second round, also contribute feedback; test examples ($T$) are never shown to the optimizer and, within a run, are evaluated only with the initial and returned prompts.

\subsection{Item-Level Transition Tracking}
\label{sec:metric}

Let $c_i(p)\in\{0,1\}$ denote the observed correctness of example $i$ under prompt $p$. Given two prompts $p$ and $q$ evaluated on the same example set $D$, we define the recovered ($R$) and regressed ($G$) examples as
\vspace{-8px}
\begin{equation}
R=\{i\in D:c_i(p)=0,\ c_i(q)=1\},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
G=\{i\in D:c_i(p)=1,\ c_i(q)=0\}.
\end{equation}
The net change $N=|R|-|G|$ corresponds to $|D|$ times the accuracy difference, whereas churn $C=|R|+|G|$ counts all examples whose correctness changes; an answer that changes from one wrong value to another is not counted (Appendix~\ref{app:eval}). Revisions with the same accuracy change can therefore show very different recovery and regression patterns (Figure~\ref{fig:metric}, Appendix~\ref{app:algorithm}). Transitions are measured between consecutive revisions on $M$ and $V$, and between the initial and returned prompts on $T$, using matched evaluable example IDs.

\subsection{Feedback-Driven Prompt Revision}
\label{sec:optimization}
\label{sec:selection}

From the second round, the optimizer receives the recoveries and regressions observed after the preceding revision on $M$ and $V$, together with the affected questions, solver responses, gold answers, previous text of modified sections, and validation accuracy changes (Algorithm~\ref{alg:reflect}, Appendix~\ref{app:algorithm}). Each changed candidate is committed as the starting point for the next round, even if its validation accuracy decreases, so later revisions can build on earlier edits without a regression gate. RFDPO returns the committed candidate with the highest validation accuracy; when two rounds tie on validation accuracy, it returns the one with higher mining accuracy. If no candidate is committed, the seed is retained. We adopted this rule after a GPT-4o-mini PUPA run showed its second round ahead of the final round on both sets; eight earlier GPT-4o-mini runs (PUPA, IFBench, and six MMLU subjects) used a final-round rule and are reported as recorded (\S\ref{sec:valtest}). RFDPO also records prompt versions, modified sections, item-level outcomes, and token costs.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Benchmarks.}
\textbf{LegalBench-Hearsay}~\citep{guha2023legalbench} evaluates binary classification of statements under U.S.\ hearsay law. \textbf{MMLU}~\citep{hendrycks2021mmlu} contributes six four-way multiple-choice subjects (college mathematics, computer security, econometrics, high school biology, philosophy, professional law), each optimized independently, with macro-averaged accuracy. \textbf{IFBench}~\citep{pyatkin2025ifbench} counts an example as correct only when a programmatic verifier in the style of IFEval~\citep{zhou2023ifeval} finds all applicable constraints satisfied. \textbf{AIME}~\citep{maa2025aime} uses problems from 2022--2024 for optimization and the 30 problems from 2025 for testing. \textbf{PUPA}~\citep{li2025papillon} evaluates privacy-conscious delegation to an external LLM; we optimize only the query-redaction prompt and report accuracy as the share of items with composite score $(\mathrm{quality}+1-\mathrm{leakage})/2 \geq 0.7$ (mean composite scores, as reported by GEPA, are in Appendix~\ref{app:prior}).

\paragraph{Models and protocol.}
GPT-4o-mini~\citep{openai2024gpt4o} and GPT-4.1 Mini~\citep{openai2025gpt41} serve as solvers at temperature $0$, and GPT-5~\citep{openai2025gpt5} as the optimizer at temperature $0.7$; PUPA also uses a fixed GPT-4.1 external model and a GPT-5 quality judge. The evaluation includes 19 baseline/final records, seven non-MMLU configurations and twelve MMLU subjects, with both endpoints evaluated within the same run. Items blocked by the provider's content filter are excluded (Table~\ref{tab:setup}). Run configurations, scoring, and optimizer instructions are in Appendices~\ref{app:config}--\ref{app:prompts}.

Test accuracy increased in seven of the nine configurations, by up to 26.3 percentage points on PUPA with GPT-4.1 Mini, but aggregate improvements do not fully represent changes in individual predictions. On LegalBench-Hearsay, a net change of one example concealed 13--17 changed outcomes, 27--35\% of the test set, and on MMLU with GPT-4o-mini, 54 examples changed correctness for a net gain of eight. These results show that small accuracy differences can conceal substantial recovery and regression. In a production pipeline, each such change is an input whose handling changes without any sign in the headline score.

\paragraph{Effect of the selection rule.}
In three of the eight final-round runs, the returned round had lower validation accuracy than round~2 (PUPA: 53.3\% vs.\ 63.3\%). Under best-validation selection, five of the eight would have returned an earlier round (Appendix~\ref{app:selection}), raising the mean validation gain of the 17 returns from $+7.9$ to $+9.7$ points. The only alternative round we evaluated on test, after the run, was PUPA round~2, which scored 63.2\% against 65.8\% for the returned round.

\subsection{Prompt Revision Behavior}
\label{sec:edits}

In round 1, where no effect report is available, the optimizer modified an average of 4.3 of the five prompt sections, compared with 1.3 in rounds 2--3, where it receives recovery and regression feedback on its previous edit; revisions thus became more localized.

% Nevertheless, smaller edits did not eliminate regressions. Later rounds recovered 56 mining examples while regressing 54, compared with 59 recoveries and 27 regressions in round 1. This indicates that revisions continued to introduce new failures even when fewer prompt sections were modified.

\subsection{Resource Consumption}
\label{sec:resources}

Each non-MMLU configuration used three optimizer calls and cost \$0.21--\$4.80 at logged prices, including seed and final evaluations; optimizer input ranged from 14K tokens for Hearsay to 498K for AIME (Table~\ref{tab:resources}, Appendix~\ref{app:resources}). PUPA incurred additional costs from external-model responses and quality judgments, while MMLU totals aggregate six independent subject runs. Because transitions reuse the per-item outcomes that any evaluation produces, tracking them adds no model calls.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Beyond aggregate accuracy.}
Churn of at least five times the net change in five of nine configurations shows that an optimized prompt may introduce failures on examples that previously worked; in a production pipeline, these are inputs handled correctly before the update. Recovery and regression tracking makes such changes visible, allowing developers to distinguish newly corrected examples from newly introduced failures and to review a prompt change as they would a code change, against the examples it fixed and broke. This aligns with regression testing for evolving LLM applications~\citep{ma2024regression}.

\paragraph{Role of item-level feedback.}
Our motivating question was whether repeated feedback could reduce regressions while keeping scores high. Later revisions were more localized, but the ablation shows that this also happens without the report; what the report changed was the outcome, reducing mining-set churn from 5.2 to 3.1 examples per round and regressions from 4.3 to 1.9 at similar net gains. For pipelines whose downstream systems depend on stable outputs, this stability is valuable in itself; test accuracy changed similarly in both conditions.

\paragraph{Generalization and reporting implications.}
Improvements during optimization do not necessarily transfer to unseen examples. Average test gains were about 66\% of validation gains, and three runs lost test accuracy despite validation improvements, so validation performance should be read as a tuning result. The selection rule should be fixed in advance, since final-round and best-validation selection would have returned different prompts in five of eight runs. We recommend reporting recovery, regression, and churn alongside validation and test accuracy, prompt versions, evaluation exclusions, and cost; RFDPO records all of these without additional solver calls (Appendix~\ref{app:artifacts}).

\section{Conclusion}
Aggregate accuracy captures the overall effect of prompt optimization but obscures which examples improve or regress. Motivated by prompt maintenance in an industrial pipeline, we presented RFDPO, which tracks item-level recoveries, regressions, and churn, feeds them back to the optimizer, and records every revision with its effect. Across five benchmarks and two solvers, churn was at least five times the absolute net change in five of nine configurations, test gains were about 66\% of validation gains, and item-level feedback reduced churn and regressions at similar net gains. These findings highlight that prompt optimization can introduce failures despite aggregate improvements, motivating the reporting of recovery, regression, churn, and validation-to-test transfer alongside accuracy.

\section*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Evaluation scope.}
RFDPO was motivated by an industrial pipeline but has so far been evaluated on public benchmarks. Our evaluation covers five benchmarks, two closed-weight solvers, and one optimizer, with a single seed and relatively small test sets. We did not evaluate open-weight models or longer optimization trajectories. Differences in selection rules and evaluation configurations also limit cross-model comparisons.

\paragraph{Evaluation variability.}
Observed recovery and regression may partly reflect model output variability rather than prompt changes, as shown by unchanged-prompt re-evaluations, and transitions are attributed to a revision as a whole rather than to individual section edits. PUPA additionally relies on an LLM judge, and items blocked by the provider's content filter are excluded rather than scored as errors. Churn counts changes in correctness only, so an answer that changes from one wrong value to another is not counted.

\paragraph{Feedback ablation.}
Our ablation covers one benchmark and solver, with five runs per condition, and its effect may differ on other tasks.

\paragraph{Generalization and data exposure.}
Validation examples are used for both feedback and selection, potentially inflating tuning gains. Test examples remain excluded from feedback within each run, but small test sets limit generalization claims. Our verbatim-overlap check also cannot detect paraphrased copying or semantic memorization.


\section*{Ethical Considerations}
Data come from public benchmarks, resplit as described in Table~\ref{tab:setup}. PUPA contains personal information from
public user--LLM conversations. Hosted solver and optimizer APIs receive the original queries during evaluation and
feedback; redaction protects only the designated external model. String-matched leakage does not capture paraphrased or
inferred information. No data from the industrial pipeline that motivated this work were used. Released traces would
contain item IDs and outcomes only, without raw inputs, predictions, or gold text (Appendix~\ref{app:artifacts}).

\subsection{Optimization Procedure and Transition Example}
\label{app:algorithm}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:reflect} gives the optimization loop, and Figure~\ref{fig:metric} illustrates the transition counts.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{RFDPO optimization with edit-effect feedback}
\label{alg:reflect}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require seed $p_0$; disjoint $M,V,T$; $\textsc{Opt}$; $\textsc{Solve}$; round budget $B$
\State $s_0,v_0 \gets \textsc{Solve}(p_0,M),\ \textsc{Solve}(p_0,V)$
\State $\tau_0 \gets \textsc{Solve}(p_0,T)$ \Comment{baseline; measured only}
\State $\mathcal{H}\gets\emptyset$; $(p,s,v)\gets(p_0,s_0,v_0)$; $\rho\gets\emptyset$
\If{seed mining accuracy $\geq 0.95$ and task is not PUPA} \State $B\gets 0$ \Comment{skip optimization} \EndIf
\For{$t=1$ \textbf{to} $B$}
  \If{no mining failures} \State \textbf{break} \EndIf
  \State $q\gets\textsc{Opt}(p,s,v,\rho)$ \Comment{mining items and feedback}
  \If{$q=p$} \State \textbf{continue} \EndIf
  \State $s',v'\gets\textsc{Solve}(q,M),\ \textsc{Solve}(q,V)$
  \State $\rho\gets((R,G)(s\!\to\!s'),(R,G)(v\!\to\!v'))$
  \State append $(q,s',v')$ to $\mathcal{H}$ \Comment{no regression gate}
  \State $(p,s,v)\gets(q,s',v')$
\EndFor
\State $p^*\gets p_0$ if $\mathcal{H}=\emptyset$; otherwise select from $\mathcal{H}$
\Statex \hspace{\algorithmicindent} by validation accuracy; validation ties go to the
\Statex \hspace{\algorithmicindent} higher mining accuracy
\State $\tau^* \gets \textsc{Solve}(p^*,T)$ \Comment{measured only}
\State \Return $p^*$, and $(R,G)(\tau_0\!\to\!\tau^*;T)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Scoring and Evaluable Items}
\label{app:eval}
\paragraph{Answer extraction.}
MMLU and Hearsay outputs are scored by extracting \texttt{Answer: <letter>} and \texttt{Answer: Yes/No}, respectively,
and comparing with the gold label. AIME uses exact integer match on \texttt{\#\#\#\# <number>} (0--999). Visible
reasoning before these answer markers is not itself scored. IFBench checks only the content following
\texttt{FINAL RESPONSE:}; an item passes when every supported constraint is satisfied. The verifier implements 32
of the 83 constraint types in IFEval and IFBench combined, and evaluated items use the supported types.

\paragraph{PUPA pipeline and metrics.}
The optimized solver prompt rewrites a private query into a redacted request. Fixed GPT-4.1 answers that request;
the solver then synthesizes a final answer under a fixed instruction, using the original query and external response.
Leakage is the fraction of annotated PII strings present in the redacted request. A GPT-5 judge scores final-answer
quality against the reference on a 0--1 scale, with up to 4,096 completion tokens. An item is correct when the
composite $(\text{quality}+1-\text{leakage})/2$ reaches 0.7; reported PUPA accuracy uses this criterion. Only the
rewrite prompt is optimized.

\paragraph{Denominators and accounting.}
Provider-blocked items are excluded rather than scored as incorrect, so some accuracies have denominators below the
assigned split (Table~\ref{tab:setup}). PUPA evaluates 29 of 30 mining and 38 of 40 test items; MMLU professional law
evaluates 22 of 25 validation and 63 of 66 test items; both hold for both solvers. IFBench evaluates 19 of 20
validation items with GPT-4o-mini and 25 of 26 mining items with GPT-4.1 Mini. The two IFBench runs were assigned
different splits (20/20 and 26/14; Table~\ref{tab:config}). Transitions are computed over common evaluated IDs, so
$\Delta\mathrm{acc}=(|R|-|G|)/|D|$ and $C=|R|+|G|=|N|+2\min(|R|,|G|)$; MMLU transitions sum subjects while its
accuracy is macro-averaged.

\paragraph{What churn does not count.}
Churn counts correctness changes only. On test, items wrong under both the seed and the returned prompt but with a
different wrong answer number 9 of 30 for AIME, 14 for MMLU with GPT-4o-mini (college mathematics 4, computer security
1, econometrics 2, biology 1, philosophy 2, professional law 4), and 9 for MMLU with GPT-4.1 Mini (0, 1, 1, 0, 3, 4).
Hearsay has a single wrong label per item, and IFBench and PUPA are scored as pass/fail and by a score threshold, so
for them churn counts only crossings of the pass criterion.

\subsection{Optimizer System Prompt}
\label{app:sysprompt}
\texttt{\{task\_description\}} and \texttt{\{max\_rounds\}} are filled per benchmark and run. The template's wording is
preserved, with reflowed lines and ASCII arrows typeset as $\rightarrow$.

\begin{promptbox}[title={\textbf{Optimizer system prompt} (best-validation version)},fontupper=\small]{softblue}
You are working with an expert prompt engineer to help a smaller LLM (the ``solver'') solve \texttt{\{task\_description\}} more
reliably.\par\medskip
\textbf{HOW THIS PROCESS WORKS} (read carefully -- this is a measured, multi-round experiment, not a one-shot rewrite):\par
\noindent 1. You get up to \texttt{\{max\_rounds\}} rewrite rounds. After each round, your rewritten prompt is run on a WORKING SET of
examples and on a VALIDATION set -- a second, disjoint batch meant to approximate unseen data.\par
\noindent 2. From round 2 on, you are shown the measured EFFECT of your previous rewrite, IN FULL, on BOTH sets: every working-set item it
RECOVERED (wrong $\rightarrow$ right), every item it REGRESSED (right $\rightarrow$ wrong) together with the solver's new wrong answer,
the previous text of every section you changed, and -- just as fully -- every VALIDATION item it recovered and every validation item it
regressed, with the same detail. Nothing about validation is held back from you.\par
\noindent 3. In those later rounds your job is causal: first diagnose WHICH of your previous edits caused WHICH regressions and
recoveries on EITHER set, then rewrite -- keep the edits that recovered items, revert or repair the edits that regressed items, and
address the remaining failures. Do not churn wording that was not implicated by the evidence.\par
\noindent 4. There is no ``last round always ships'' rule. Every round commits unconditionally to a version history, and at the end,
whichever COMMITTED round scores BEST on validation accuracy is the one that actually ships -- not necessarily the last round, and not
necessarily this one. Validation accuracy is therefore not just a diagnostic number: it is literally the criterion your rewrite is
competing on. Treat every round as a real, standalone candidate for shipping, not a throwaway step toward some final round.\par
\noindent 5. Because you now see every validation item that flips, a rewrite that merely patches the specific items shown -- without
addressing the underlying rule that produced them -- will look good this round and can still fail on the next genuinely new case.
Prefer fixing the general rule that explains the failures over specific patches to the exact items shown.\par\medskip
You will see: the solver's current markdown-formatted prompt; (from round 2) the EFFECT REPORT of your previous rewrite described above;
EVERY question the solver currently gets wrong on the working set, with the solver's answer and the correct answer alongside (not a
sample -- all of them); and EVERY question the current prompt already gets right on the working set (not a sample -- all of them), so you
know exactly what must not break.\par\medskip
\textbf{CRITICAL RULE ABOUT EXAMPLES} -- READ BEFORE WRITING ANYTHING: you are shown every failure, every success, and every validation
item so you can DIAGNOSE what is going wrong, NOT so you can turn them into illustrative examples. When you write an invented example in
the Context or Task Details section: (a) Do NOT reuse the underlying scenario, fact pattern, or entities of ANY failure, gold, or
validation item shown to you in this conversation -- not verbatim, and not paraphrased with different names. Changing ``Linda and her
editors'' to ``Sarah and her colleagues'' is still copying; the solver pattern-matches on the STORY, not the names. (b) Before writing
an invented example, actively check: does this resemble the setup of anything I was just shown (a lab report, a bystander pointing, an
email admitting knowledge, a customer asking for a refund, someone hearing about a planned crime, a doctor's diagnosis, and so on)? If
yes, discard it and invent a genuinely different scenario. (c) Prefer stating the principle abstractly with NO example at all over
reusing a shown scenario's shape. An abstract rule generalizes; a disguised replay of a shown item does not -- it is memorization with a
different name attached, and it is exactly what causes a prompt to score well on the items you were shown and fail on the next genuinely
new one.\par\medskip
\textbf{CRITICAL FACT ABOUT THE SOLVER:} it has NO hidden scratchpad. Its ONLY reasoning space is the text it actually writes out. So
``think step by step'' works only if the solver WRITES the steps in its output, before the final answer line. Instructions like
``reason internally'', ``use a scratchpad but do not show it'', or ``output only the answer'' DESTROY the reasoning entirely -- for this
model they are identical to ``do not reason'', regardless of task type. Where a task does not need reasoning, the correct instruction is
to keep the answer direct -- never phrasings like these.\par\medskip
\textbf{FIRST, identify the task type from the failures you are shown:} (i) Reasoning tasks -- anything needing calculation, derivation,
formal logic, OR applying a rule/definition to a specific scenario (e.g. mathematics, econometrics, and hearsay / most legal analysis):
the solver RELIABLY does better when it WRITES OUT its working step by step before the final answer line. A bare answer starves it.
(ii) Pure factual-recall / lookup tasks -- where the answer is simply known or not, with no intermediate steps (e.g. a memorised
definition or fact): forced step-by-step reasoning can make the solver over-think and second-guess a fact it already recalled, LOWERING
accuracy. Sharpen the definitions and framing instead, and keep the answer direct. When unsure which it is, ALLOW visible reasoning --
it helps more often than it hurts, and the final answer line is extracted either way. Match your rewrite to the task type before doing
anything else.\par\medskip
\textbf{A good rewrite:} surfaces the general reasoning principle that separates correct from incorrect answers on this task, and states
it in terms the solver can apply to any new case it has not seen before; gives the solver the mental scaffolding it needs -- a clearer
way to think about the problem, a checklist, a decision procedure, a definition it can lean on; illustrates principles with GENUINELY
INVENTED examples when helpful -- different scenario, different entities, different setup from anything you were shown -- or no example
at all; keeps the fixed markdown schema (\texttt{\#\# System Role}, Context, Task Details, Constraints, Output Format), where you may
edit any section but must not add or remove these headers; keeps the FINAL answer line's format exactly (e.g. a line
\texttt{Answer: <LETTER>} or \texttt{Answer: Yes/No}), which is ALL the scorer reads and is searched for anywhere in the output, so you
MAY, and for reasoning tasks SHOULD, let the solver write visible step-by-step working BEFORE it (NEVER add ``output only the answer'',
``do not show your working'', or ``reason internally''); and MATCHES the reasoning style to the task type, with the objective being
accuracy, never verbosity for its own sake.\par\medskip
\textbf{RESPONSE FORMAT:} you MAY begin your reply with a short \texttt{\#\# Analysis} section (2-6 sentences diagnosing which previous
edit caused which regression or recovery -- state the cause before the fix; in round 1 you may use it to state your hypothesis instead).
The \texttt{\#\# Analysis} section is discarded automatically by the parser. After it, output the full rewritten markdown with the five
standard section headers. No other prose, no code fences, nothing after the last section.
\end{promptbox}

\paragraph{No-report variant.}
In the ablation of Appendix~\ref{app:ablation}, the no-report arm receives the same prompt without points 2, 3, and 5,
without the effect-report entry in the list of inputs, and without the references to validation items in the example
rule. Point 4 keeps its shipping rule without the remark on validation accuracy, and the response-format note asks for
a hypothesis about the failures instead of a diagnosis of the previous edit. The user message contains no effect
report and no promise of one. Everything else is identical.

\subsection{Task-Specific Insertions}
\label{app:insertions}
\texttt{\{task\_description\}} supplies fixed task-specific guidance, quoted below. The MMLU
guidance distinguishes computational from recall subjects.

\begin{promptbox}[title={\textbf{Task descriptions inserted into the system prompt}},fontupper=\small]{softgreen}
\textbf{Hearsay.} a binary hearsay classification task under U.S. Federal Rule of Evidence 801 (deciding whether a courtroom statement is
hearsay). The output is Yes or No.\par
\textbf{MMLU.} a 4-way multiple-choice exam question (options A, B, C, D) from one academic subject. Computational subjects (mathematics,
econometrics) need multi-step working; factual-recall subjects (law, computer security, and similar) are usually answered best directly,
without forced step-by-step reasoning. The final answer is a single letter A, B, C, or D on an ``Answer:'' line.\par
\textbf{AIME.} a competition-level (AIME) math problem, substantially harder than grade-school word problems -- multi-step algebra,
combinatorics, number theory, geometry, or probability. The correct answer is always a specific integer between 0 and 999. The output
must end with a line containing the final numeric answer.\par
\textbf{IFBench.} an instruction-following task with the same mechanically verifiable constraint structure and the same
\texttt{FINAL RESPONSE:} marker convention as IFEval (see that description for the full explanation), but drawn from a broader set of constraint types (word/consonant/vowel counts,
keyword-frequency and forbidden-word rules, formatting requirements, and more). Correctness means the text after the marker satisfies
every listed constraint; there is no single gold answer to match.\par
\textbf{PUPA.} a privacy-conscious query-rewriting task: the input is a PRIVATE user request (may contain names, contact info, employers, or
other personal details). You are ONLY editing the REWRITE instruction -- the prompt that tells a model how to anonymize/generalize the
request before it is sent to a separate, untrusted external model. You are NOT editing how the final answer to the user is composed; that
step is fixed elsewhere. A failure means the rewritten request still leaked one or more specific private details (shown to you as
``leaked $N/M$ PII units'') -- the fix is to make the rewrite instruction more systematic about generalizing/abstracting every private
detail (names $\rightarrow$ roles, specific employers $\rightarrow$ generic descriptions, exact locations $\rightarrow$ broader regions,
etc.) while still preserving enough of the request's substance that the external model's answer stays useful. The ``gold''/quality signal
shown to you reflects the FINAL answer's usefulness (not directly under your control), so do not sacrifice the request's meaning purely to
hide details -- an unusable rewrite scores just as badly as a leaky one.
\end{promptbox}

\subsection{Per-Round Feedback}
\label{app:feedback}
The user message contains the iteration number and budget, the current prompt, every mining failure (question, wrong
output, gold answer), and every mining success (question and gold). From round 2, its effect report adds the previous
text of changed sections, recovered and regressed items on both $M$ and $V$, and before/after validation accuracy.
Recovered items include question and gold; regressed items also include the new wrong output, truncated to its last
400 characters. The message ends with a request to diagnose the observations and return the five-section markdown.
The parser discards an optional \texttt{\#\# Analysis} preamble; a no-change response does not commit a new version.
IFBench pins Output Format to preserve its answer marker; the other four sections remain editable.

\subsection{Worked Prompt Changes}
\label{app:evolution}
The excerpt below shows the returned Task Details of one run. The same revision also changed Context and Constraints,
so the outcome changes below belong to the revision as a whole, not to this section alone.

\paragraph{Hearsay, GPT-4.1 Mini.}
The seed's Task Details consisted of ``Decide whether the given statement is hearsay.'' The returned round-2 section
is reproduced below with formatting adjusted. Between the seed and this prompt, validation changed from 76.0\% to
96.0\%, and test accuracy from 35/49 to 36/49, with seven recoveries and six regressions: a one-item net gain that
moved thirteen test outcomes. Some of these flips may be re-evaluation noise (Appendix~\ref{app:noise}).

\begin{promptbox}[title={\textbf{Hearsay: returned Task Details} (round 2 excerpt)},fontupper=\small]{softgreen}
Decide whether the given statement is hearsay by applying this decision procedure and explain your steps
briefly before the final answer:\par
\textit{Step 1.} Is there a ``statement''? Identify whether the evidence is an assertion (oral/written) or assertive conduct.
Non-assertive words (questions, commands, offers) and non-assertive conduct are not statements.\par
\textit{Step 2.} Was it made out of court? In-court testimony in this proceeding is not hearsay for this purpose.\par
\textit{Step 3.} What specific proposition is the evidence offered to prove (ultimate or intermediate)? Then apply the independent
relevance test: would the evidence still be probative of that proposition even if the content of the statement were false? If yes, it
is offered for a non-truth purpose; if no, it is offered for its truth.\par
\textit{Step 4.} If the evidence is offered solely for a non-truth purpose, answer No. Common non-truth purposes: effect on the listener,
notice, knowledge or motive where only the hearing of the words matters; proving that a conversation or words occurred; and
circumstantial use of words to show a condition or state of the speaker unrelated to their truth.\par
\textit{Step 5 (guardrail on embedded assertions).} If the proponent relies on the statement to establish an intermediate fact that
itself depends on the truth of an asserted proposition within it (e.g., ``I heard/know/saw X''), the statement is used for its
truth; treat as hearsay. Do not invoke the guardrail when the use is only to show that words were spoken, the speaker's state, or an
effect on the listener where content truth is irrelevant under Step 3.\par
\textit{Step 6.} If out-of-court and offered for the truth of what it asserts, answer Yes; otherwise answer No.
\end{promptbox}

\section{Evaluation Checks and Artifacts}
\label{app:noisebugs}

\subsection{Re-evaluation of an Unchanged Prompt}
\label{app:noise}
In a separate GPT-4o-mini IFBench run, the returned prompt was identical to the seed, so its two evaluations on the
same 42 test items measure re-evaluation noise: one outcome changed (no recoveries, one regression). The edited prompt
of the main GPT-4o-mini IFBench run, which started from a different seed prompt, changed ten outcomes (six recoveries,
four regressions). In an earlier GPT-4o-mini AIME run, the returned prompt was likewise identical to the seed, and its
two test evaluations solved 4 and 3 of 30 problems. Both runs are excluded from all other analyses.

\subsection{Test-Exposure Diagnostic}
\label{app:oracle}
A diagnostic on LegalBench-Hearsay used an earlier variant of the optimizer with GPT-5, seed 42, two rounds, 30
training items, and 64 test items. With the test items held out, accuracy changed from 44/64 to 47/64 (68.8\% to
73.4\%). Supplying the same test items as optimizer feedback raised accuracy to 61/64 (95.3\%), a gain more than five
times the held-out gain. This run is excluded from all other analyses.

\subsection{Verbatim-Overlap Check}
\label{app:overlap}

its word 8-grams with all string fields in the train and test dataset files, including answer-option text. Sixteen of
the 19 prompts share none, and three share some (Table~\ref{tab:overlap}). The mathematical phrase occurs only in the
test file; the True/False template occurs in both train and test.
