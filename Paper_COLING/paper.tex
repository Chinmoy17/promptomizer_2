\begin{abstract}
Automatic prompt optimization (APO) improves task performance by iteratively rewriting prompts from model-generated
feedback, yet its evaluation is typically reduced to a single before/after score. Such aggregate scores obscure which
examples an edit recovers, which it regresses, and whether gains observed during optimization transfer to unseen data.
We introduce Reflective FDPO, an auditable prompt optimization framework that records item-level recoveries and
regressions for every candidate edit and returns this evidence to the optimizing model before subsequent rewrites. This
lets the optimizer reason not only about whether a prompt improved, but also about where and how its behavior changed.
Across five benchmark families and two solver models (20 optimization runs, counting MMLU subjects separately), we
observe substantial item-level changes even when aggregate differences are small: edits frequently recover some examples
while regressing others. Validation accuracy rose in 12 of the 17 runs that shipped a prompt, yet the sealed-test score
fell in 4 of those 12; on the eight non-MMLU runs the mean validation gain (+15.0 points) was about twice the mean test
gain (+7.8 points), so gains obtained during optimization do not transfer proportionally to unseen data. Outcomes also
vary across tasks and solvers, with improvements on some and unchanged or worse test scores on others. Our experiments
are small and single-seed, so we offer them as evidence for a reporting practice rather than a new state of the art. We
release the optimizer and per-item optimization traces, and argue that recovery, regression, and validation-to-test
transfer should be first-class reporting dimensions for automatic prompt optimization.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Small changes in prompt wording can shift task accuracy
substantially~\citep{sclar2024quantifying}, and tuning prompts by hand does not
scale across tasks and models. Automatic prompt optimization (APO) addresses
this by letting an LLM iteratively rewrite a prompt using feedback on its
failures. Across this literature the evaluation convention is nearly uniform:
one aggregate accuracy before optimization, one after, and their difference.

That convention hides two things a practitioner needs to know. First, the
aggregate delta cannot separate an edit that fixed several items and broke none
from one that fixed the same number of items while silently breaking others. On
the small test sets common in this literature the difference is large: in our
experiments, a net change of one test item often coexisted with a dozen or more
items flipping in opposite directions (\S\ref{sec:results}). Second, an
aggregate gain measured on the data that guided the optimization says little
about unseen data once the optimizer, or the person running it, has selected
the best of several candidates against that same data.

A human prompt engineer reviewing an edit asks what it fixed and what it broke
before making the next one. We ask whether an optimizer LLM behaves usefully
when given the same view. We therefore (i) formalize \emph{per-round
recovery/regression tracking}, an item-level record of every edit, and (ii) build
\emph{Reflective FDPO}, a deliberately simple optimizer that returns this record to
the optimizing LLM before its next rewrite (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}). We use the
system as a testbed on five benchmarks that differ in task type, answer format,
and metric: LegalBench-Hearsay, MMLU, IFBench/IFEval, AIME, and PUPA, with two
inexpensive solver models, GPT-4o-mini and GPT-4.1 Mini.

Our scale is small by design: tens of test items per benchmark, a single seed,
and at most three optimizer calls per run. We therefore make no leaderboard
claim and do not compare numerically against published systems, whose
train/validation/test scale, scaffolding, and solver models differ from ours
(\S\ref{sec:related}). Our claims concern what item-level tracking reveals
\emph{within} each run, and they are the following.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A reusable accounting.} Per-round recovery/regression tracking with
explicit definitions, including \emph{churn}, the number of items that flip in either
direction, which the net accuracy change conceals (\S\ref{sec:metric}).
\item \textbf{A testbed optimizer.} Reflective FDPO: a five-section prompt schema,
unconditional per-round commits, selection among rounds by validation accuracy, and a
sealed test set that is only ever scored and never used for any decision (\S\ref{sec:method}).
\item \textbf{A quantified validation/test gap.} Validation rose in 12 of 17
shipped runs while the test score fell in 4 of those 12; on the non-MMLU runs validation gains
overstate test gains by roughly 2x on average; and a verbatim-overlap check rules out
copying as the explanation (\S\ref{sec:valtest}).
\item \textbf{Practical evidence for the lens.} Item-level traces exposed a truncated
judge and a mis-specified constraint checker in our own harness, and showed that
identical aggregate scores can hide different item sets (\S\ref{sec:noise},
\S\ref{sec:bugs}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Prompt optimization.}
APO has progressed from discrete token-level search~\citep{shin2020autoprompt}
and generate-and-score instruction search~\citep{zhou2023ape}, to LLMs used as
optimizers over trajectories of scored prompts~\citep{yang2024opro}, textual-gradient
edits~\citep{pryzant2023protegi,yuksekgonul2025textgrad}, evolutionary
search~\citep{guo2024evoprompt,fernando2024promptbreeder}, and multi-stage pipelines
optimized jointly over instructions and demonstrations~\citep{opsahlong2024miprov2,schnabel2024sammo}, many of them packaged in
programming frameworks such as DSPy~\citep{khattab2024dspy}.
Closest to our prompt schema is MPO~\citep{sharma2026mpo}, which decomposes a prompt
into the same five semantic sections and applies section-local textual gradients;
its updates are additive and de-duplicated, without a check that a rewrite does not break a
previously correct case. Recovery/regression accounting is that missing check, applied as
a measurement rather than a gate. GEPA~\citep{agrawal2026gepa} combines reflective
prompt mutation with Pareto-based selection over a population of prompts, and
Trace2Policy~\citep{zha2026trace2policy} refines a rule document by clustering errors and committing
patches that pass a regression gate. General self-feedback methods return verbal critiques to the
model that produced an output~\citep{madaan2023selfrefine,shinn2023reflexion}. Reflective FDPO differs in
what the optimizer is shown: a measured, item-level outcome of its own previous edit rather than a
critique or a scalar score.

\paragraph{Why we do not compare numerically.}
Published APO results use train, validation, and test sets and solver models that differ from ours by
an order of magnitude in evaluation size, often with scaffolded baselines, so their absolute deltas
are not commensurable with deltas measured on 30 to 66 test items. We cite such systems for
their mechanisms only, and we restrict our empirical claims to comparisons made inside our own runs.

\paragraph{Evaluation beyond accuracy.}
Our framing, that one aggregate number hides qualitatively different behaviors, follows
CheckList~\citep{ribeiro2020checklist}, which showed that models passing standard
benchmarks fail systematically on targeted tests. CheckList evaluates a fixed trained
model; we instead track per-item outcomes across the successive edits of an optimization
process. The validation/test gap we measure is an instance of adaptive overfitting from repeated
selection against a fixed set, which~\citet{recht2019imagenet} documented for ImageNet
classifiers without any data contamination. It is distinct from test-set memorization, which
\citet{oren2023proving} show can be detected in black-box language models; we check for
verbatim copying directly (\S\ref{sec:valtest}).

\paragraph{Test-set exposure.}
Trace2Policy's own appendix reports that one refinement round for its headline LegalBench-Hearsay
result was diagnosed partly from the nominally held-out test set~\citep{zha2026trace2policy}.
To measure how sensitive this task is to such exposure, we ran an earlier, simpler variant of our optimizer
with the sealed test pool deliberately used as the mining set (Claude Haiku 4.5 \citep{anthropic2025haiku45} as solver, outside the main
experiments). It moved accuracy from 68.8\% to 95.3\%. This diagnostic is invalid as a result and we report it
only as a caution: exposure alone can produce gains of this size regardless of mechanism, which is
why every other number in this paper uses a sealed test set.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Setting}
A prompt is a document with five fixed sections (System Role, Context, Task Details,
Constraints, Output Format), so edits are always well formed and comparable across rounds. A
\emph{solver} LLM answers items using the prompt; an \emph{optimizer} LLM rewrites the prompt. Each benchmark's
data is split into three disjoint pools: a \emph{mining} set $M$ whose failures the optimizer may see, a
\emph{validation} set $V$ used to select among rounds and, from round 2, also shown to the optimizer as
feedback (so $V$ is a second tuning set, not held-out data), and a sealed \emph{test} set $T$. The solver is
never the optimizer or the judge.

\subsection{Recovery/regression tracking}
\label{sec:metric}
Let $c_i(p)\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether the solver answers item $i$ correctly under prompt $p$ (for
PUPA, whether the composite score passes a fixed threshold of 0.7). For two prompts $p,q$ and an item set $D$:
\begin{align}
R(p{\to}q;D) &= \{i\in D : c_i(p)=0,\ c_i(q)=1\} \\
G(p{\to}q;D) &= \{i\in D : c_i(p)=1,\ c_i(q)=0\} \\
N &= |R|-|G| = |D|\,(\mathrm{acc}_D(q)-\mathrm{acc}_D(p)) \\
C &= |R|+|G| \quad\text{(churn)}
\end{align}
$R$ and $G$ are the \emph{recovered} and \emph{regressed} items. The net change $N$ is
exactly the accuracy change, so two edits with the same $N$ are indistinguishable by accuracy even
when their churn $C$ differs greatly. We compute $(R,G)$ between consecutive rounds on $M$ and on $V$, and once
on $T$ between the seed prompt and the shipped prompt. All sets are stored per item, so
any aggregate in this paper can be audited down to the individual items that moved.

\subsection{Reflective FDPO}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:reflect} gives the procedure and Figure~\ref{fig:arch} its data flow. Round
$t=1$ has nothing to reflect on. From round 2 the optimizer is shown, in full and without sampling, the items its
previous rewrite recovered and regressed on both $M$ and $V$ (the question, the solver's new wrong answer, and the gold
answer), the previous text of every section it changed, and the validation accuracy before and after. It is also told
that the best committed round by validation accuracy is the one that ships (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}). Every round
commits unconditionally, so a poor round cannot block a later good one, and the shipped prompt is the committed round
with the highest validation accuracy (ties broken by mining accuracy). Nine early GPT-4o-mini runs (AIME, PUPA, IFBench, and
all six MMLU subjects) predate this rule and shipped the final round instead (Appendix~\ref{app:gap}). Optimization is skipped when the seed already
exceeds 95\% accuracy on $M$, and a round that proposes no change is skipped. The optimizer is instructed not to copy
dataset items into the prompt (\S\ref{app:history}). Appendix~\ref{app:design} gives, for each design decision, the
alternative we rejected and the values from our saved runs that support the choice.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Reflective FDPO}
\label{alg:reflect}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require seed prompt $p_0$; disjoint sets $M, V, T$; optimizer $\textsc{Opt}$; solver $\textsc{Solve}$; rounds $N$
\State $s_0,v_0 \gets \textsc{Solve}(p_0,M),\ \textsc{Solve}(p_0,V)$
\State $\tau_0 \gets \textsc{Solve}(p_0,T)$ \Comment{baseline; measured only}
\For{$t=1$ \textbf{to} $N$}
  \If{$t=1$} $\rho \gets \emptyset$
  \Else
    \State $(R_M,G_M) \gets (R,G)(s_{t-2}\!\to\! s_{t-1};M)$
    \State $(R_V,G_V) \gets (R,G)(v_{t-2}\!\to\! v_{t-1};V)$
    \State $\rho \gets (R_M,G_M,R_V,G_V)$
  \EndIf
  \State $p_t \gets \textsc{Opt}(p_{t-1},\{i\in M: s_{t-1,i}=0\},\rho)$
  \State $s_t,v_t \gets \textsc{Solve}(p_t,M),\ \textsc{Solve}(p_t,V)$
  \State save $(p_t,s_t,v_t)$ \Comment{always; no gate}
\EndFor
\State $t^*\gets \arg\max_{1\le t\le N}\mathrm{acc}(v_t)$; $\ p^*\gets p_{t^*}$
\State $\tau^* \gets \textsc{Solve}(p^*,T)$ \Comment{measured only}
\State \Return $p^*$, and $(R,G)(\tau_0\!\to\!\tau^*;T)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Sealed-test protocol}
$T$ is scored exactly twice per run: with $p_0$ before optimization, and with $p^*$ after. Those two scores
define the baseline and final numbers reported everywhere in this paper. $T$ is never shown to the
optimizer and is never used to select a round, edit a prompt, or stop a run.

\paragraph{Models.}
Solvers are GPT-4o-mini \citep{openai2024gpt4o} and GPT-4.1 Mini \citep{openai2025gpt41} (temperature 0). The optimizer is GPT-5 \citep{openai2025gpt5} (temperature 0.7). PUPA
additionally uses GPT-5 as its quality judge and a fixed, never-optimized GPT-4.1 \citep{openai2025gpt41} as the untrusted external model
in its two-stage pipeline. The solver is never the optimizer or the judge, and the PUPA external model differs from the solver.
Scoring details are in Appendix~\ref{app:eval} and the optimizer's instructions in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
Each (benchmark, solver) pair is run once, with seed 0 and up to three optimization rounds (two optimizer calls
in one case where mining failures ran out). Splits are class-stratified (Hearsay, IFBench, PUPA), subject-balanced (MMLU), or
fixed by year (AIME). We hold per-benchmark configuration constant across solvers (Appendix~\ref{app:config}, Table~\ref{tab:config}).
MMLU subjects are optimized independently, giving 12 runs. Counting those, 20 optimization runs underlie
\S\ref{sec:valtest}. A single GPT-4.1 Mini run costs between \$0.01 and \$4.80 in API fees.

Validation accuracy rose in 12 of the 17 shipped runs, and in 4 of those 12 the test score fell: AIME with
GPT-4.1 Mini (validation $+6.2$, test $-3.3$), IFBench with GPT-4o-mini ($+15.8$, $-2.4$), Hearsay with GPT-4o-mini
($+12.0$, $-2.0$), and MMLU computer security with GPT-4o-mini ($+8.0$, $-1.5$). On the eight non-MMLU runs the
mean validation gain is about twice the mean test gain. This is the expected signature of tuning against a small set
(14 to 32 items here, so one item is 3 to 7 points): $V$ feeds the optimizer from round 2 on (\S\ref{sec:method}), so it is a
second tuning set rather than held-out data, and in the 11 runs that used best-of-rounds selection it also chooses the
shipped round. The gap appears under both shipping rules (Table~\ref{tab:gap}) and is larger for the final-round runs
(5.3 points) than for the best-of-rounds runs (1.3 points), so round selection alone does not explain it; with 8 and 9
shipped runs and different benchmark mixes we draw no further conclusion. It matches the adaptive
overfitting described by~\citet{recht2019imagenet}, scaled down to a single run. Two shipped MMLU prompts also scored
\emph{below} the seed on validation (college mathematics with GPT-4o-mini and computer security with GPT-4.1 Mini),
because unconditional commits do not require beating the seed (Tables~\ref{tab:pergap} and~\ref{tab:mmlu}).

\paragraph{Not memorization.}
We checked the final prompt of every run (the seed itself when nothing shipped) for verbatim copying by counting 8-word sequences shared with any item in the
train or test file of the corresponding dataset. In 17 of 20 runs the overlap is zero. The remaining three share one
generic mathematical phrase (MMLU college mathematics, GPT-4o-mini) and a boilerplate pattern of True/False answer
options (MMLU computer security, both solvers). Copying therefore does not explain the gap (Appendix~\ref{app:overlap}). The check cannot detect
paraphrased scenarios, which is why the optimizer is explicitly told never to reuse the scenario of any shown item
(Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}); we claim only the absence of verbatim copying.

\subsection{Evaluation noise}
\label{sec:noise}
Item-level traces also show how much of a score is noise. On IFBench, two GPT-4o-mini runs with identical configuration
ended at the same aggregate (47.6 $\rightarrow$ 45.2) but with different item sets: 2 recovered/3 regressed in one, 0/1 in the other.
On AIME with GPT-4.1 Mini, the unchanged seed prompt scored 10, 14, and 16 of 30 in three successive runs
(which also changed the generation-token cap and fixed one gold label), and the final shipped prompt scored 15, 11, and 14
of 30 when evaluated three times on the same test set. A spread of five items (16.7 points) across evaluations of a single fixed
prompt exceeds every AIME delta in Table~\ref{tab:main}, so we regard the AIME results as inconclusive, and we report
the saved run (53.3 $\rightarrow$ 50.0) rather than a hand-selected baseline.

\subsection{What the tracking caught in our own harness}
\label{sec:bugs}
\paragraph{PUPA judge truncation.}
The PUPA quality judge is a reasoning model whose completions were being silently truncated by a hard-coded
token cap unrelated to the experiment's solver budget; truncated calls defaulted to a quality score of zero. The
item-level trace flagged a pattern of validation regressions that did not correspond to any difference in prompt content,
and following the affected items back to the judge's token usage located the cap. After the fix, GPT-4.1 Mini's PUPA composite
rose from 68.2 to 80.7 with 10 test items recovered and none regressed.

\paragraph{IFBench checker and stale optimizer instruction.}
A regression the trace marked as suspicious led us to a constraint rule (\texttt{words:no\_consecutive}) that
checked a different condition than the one stated in the instruction text. A separate audit found an optimizer
instruction that no longer matched the round-selection logic. After fixing both, IFBench with GPT-4.1 Mini moved from 42.9
to 66.7 (12 recovered, 2 regressed). The GPT-4o-mini IFBench and PUPA runs predate these fixes, so Table~\ref{tab:main}
should be compared within a solver, not across solvers.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{What we recommend reporting.}
For any prompt optimizer: (i) recovered, regressed, and churn counts on the sealed test set, not only the net change;
(ii) the validation gain next to the test change, since selection inflates the former; and (iii) the score of the
\emph{same} prompt under repeated evaluation, so readers can see the noise floor. All three are cheap given per-item logs.

\paragraph{What we do not show.}
Our results do not isolate whether showing the optimizer its own recovered/regressed record improves optimization:
we ran no matched ablation against failure-only feedback. The recovery/regression accounting is the contribution we
can support; Reflective FDPO is the instrument that produced the evidence, and its behavior across solvers (IFBench helps
GPT-4.1 Mini and hurts GPT-4o-mini) is reported as observed.

\section{Conclusion}
Per-item recovery/regression tracking turns an aggregate accuracy delta into a record of what an optimization actually
changed. On five benchmarks and two solvers it showed large churn behind small net changes, validation gains that
overstate test gains by about a factor of two on the non-MMLU runs, and bugs in our own evaluation harness. We urge future prompt-optimization work to report
these quantities, and we release all per-item traces to make that easy (Appendix~\ref{app:artifacts}).

\section*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\paragraph{Scale and noise.} Each (benchmark, solver) pair has one seed and a test set of 30 to 66 items, so one item is worth
1.5 to 3.3 points and most deltas are within noise; we report no confidence intervals. AIME, where repeated evaluations of
one prompt differ by five items, is the clearest case.
\paragraph{No method comparison.} We do not compare numerically against published systems, whose data scale and
scaffolding differ, and we make no state-of-the-art claim. We also ran no ablation isolating the reflection signal.
\paragraph{Fix-state asymmetry.} GPT-4.1 Mini runs on PUPA, IFBench, and AIME were measured after harness fixes that
GPT-4o-mini runs predate. Nine GPT-4o-mini runs also shipped the final round rather than the best-validation round, because
they predate that rule. Compare within a solver.
\paragraph{Development used test-time traces.} The harness decisions described in \S\ref{sec:noise} and
\S\ref{sec:bugs} (the AIME token cap, the PUPA judge limit, the IFBench checker) were made after inspecting traces that included
test items. These changed how outputs are generated or scored, not which items the optimizer could see, but $T$ was not entirely
outside the development loop for the harness.
\paragraph{Mechanism.} The shipped prompt need not beat the seed on validation (2 of 17 did not). The optimizer sees flipped
validation items and is told that validation accuracy decides which round ships, so $V$ is not held out and the
validation gains in Table~\ref{tab:gap} are optimistic by construction. Our IFBench verifier implements
a subset (32 of 83) of the constraint types in IFEval and IFBench combined, and PUPA scores depend on an LLM judge.
\paragraph{Models.} Only two closed-weight solvers and one optimizer/judge (GPT-5) were used, and API behavior can drift. We did not evaluate open-weight solvers.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}
All data are public benchmarks used as released. PUPA is built from public user--LLM conversations and contains
personally identifiable information by design; we use it only to score privacy-preserving behavior. Released per-item
traces contain item identifiers and outcomes, not item text, and raw model-call logs are not released. Optimization uses
commercial APIs, which has monetary but small environmental cost at our scale (\$0.01 to \$4.80 per run).

\subsection{Reporting churn}
\label{app:d1}
\paragraph{Proposition 1.} For prompts $p,q$ and an item set $D$, let $N=|R|-|G|$ and $C=|R|+|G|$ as in
\S\ref{sec:metric}. Then $\mathrm{acc}_D(q)-\mathrm{acc}_D(p)=N/|D|$, $C=|N|+2\min(|R|,|G|)$, and $|N|\le C\le |D|$ with
$C\equiv N \pmod 2$. Every such pair $(N,C)$ is attained by some pattern of outcomes.

\emph{Proof.} $R$ and $G$ are disjoint subsets of $D$, so $|R|+|G|\le|D|$. Items with $c_i(p)=c_i(q)$ contribute equally to both
accuracies, so the accuracy difference is $(|R|-|G|)/|D|$. Writing $C-|N|=(|R|+|G|)-||R|-|G||=2\min(|R|,|G|)$ gives the identity. For
any integers $R,G\ge 0$ with $R+G\le |D|$ an outcome pattern with $|R|=R$ and $|G|=G$ exists, and $R=(C+N)/2$, $G=(C-N)/2$ are
non-negative integers exactly when $C\ge|N|$ and $C\equiv N \pmod 2$. \hfill$\square$

\paragraph{Finding.} The net change constrains churn only from below. For Hearsay with GPT-4o-mini, $N=-1$ over $|D|=49$ permits
any odd $C$ from 1 to 49; the observed value is 17 (8 recovered, 9 regressed). Reporting $N$ alone would not distinguish this run from
one that changed a single item.

\subsection{Committing every round}
\label{app:d2}
Two retired designs used a regression gate (Appendix~\ref{app:history}): one rejected over 40\% of proposed edits with no improvement
trend, and one rejected net-positive trades outright. In the final runs, 8 of the 23 committed rounds in Table~\ref{tab:rounds} lowered
validation accuracy relative to the previous round. The clearest case is Hearsay with GPT-4o-mini, where round 1 lowered validation
accuracy from 68.0 to 44.0 (seven items regressed) and rounds 2 and 3 reached 72.0 and 80.0, the run's best. A gate that rejected
round 1 would have discarded the lineage that produced the shipped prompt, although we cannot say what the later rounds would have
produced from the seed.
\paragraph{Finding.} Unconditional commits keep every lineage reachable. The cost is that the shipped prompt can score below the seed on
validation: 2 of 17 shipped runs did.

\subsection{Selection rule}
\label{app:d3}
The shipped round is the committed round with the highest validation accuracy, ties broken by mining accuracy. Among the 11 runs with a
unique best-validation round, that round was round 1 in 2 runs, round 2 in 6, and round 3 in 3, so the last round was the unique best in
only 3. Nine early GPT-4o-mini runs shipped the last round instead (\S\ref{sec:method}); in 4 of them the shipped round scored below an
earlier round on validation, by 3.1 (AIME), 10.0 (PUPA), 15.8 (IFBench), and 4.5 (MMLU professional law) points, and in the other five
the last round was best or tied. Under the best-of-rounds rule the shipped round beat the last round on validation by 9.4 (AIME,
GPT-4.1 Mini), 12.0 (Hearsay, GPT-4.1 Mini), 4.0 (MMLU philosophy, GPT-4.1 Mini), and 13.6 (MMLU professional law, GPT-4.1 Mini)
points, and tied in the other five. Validation accuracy tied between rounds in 7 of the 18 runs that optimized, which is common when $V$
has 14 to 32 items, so the tie-break matters: in IFBench with GPT-4.1 Mini, rounds 1 and 3 tied at 71.4 and round 1 shipped on mining accuracy
(52.0 against 40.0).
\paragraph{Finding.} Validation accuracy is the selection criterion, so these gaps are non-negative by construction and are not evidence
of better test performance; the test comparison by rule in Table~\ref{tab:gap} is confounded by model and benchmark mix.

\subsection{Whole-prompt rewrite in a fixed schema}
\label{app:d4}
The optimizer returns the full markdown prompt, which the parser reads section by section. This replaced exact-substring edits, which
failed in repeated runs (Appendix~\ref{app:history}). Across all 53 optimizer calls in the 20 runs, none failed to parse and none omitted a
section; one call proposed no change and the round was skipped. Committed rounds edited a mean of 4.1 of the 5 sections in round 1
($n=18$) and 1.3 in rounds 2 and 3 ($n=34$; 23 of 34 edited a single section). Over all 52 committed rounds, Task Details was edited 50
times, Context 25, Constraints 21, System Role 12, and Output Format 11.
\paragraph{Finding.} The schema is well formed in every call, and edits concentrate in Task Details. The narrowing after round 1 is
confounded with later rounds having fewer failures left to address, so we do not attribute it to the effect report.

\subsection{Showing every item}
\label{app:d5}
The optimizer sees every failure and every success on $M$, and every flipped item on $V$, with no sampling, so it knows what must not break.
This is affordable at our scale. Mean optimizer input per call was 5.2K tokens (4.7K to 5.7K) for Hearsay, 7.3K (6.7K to 7.8K) for
IFBench, 6.8K (4.6K to 10.9K) for MMLU, 23.7K (22.8K to 24.6K) for PUPA, and 180K (166K to 195K) for AIME, where each solver completion
is long. Solver output shown to the optimizer is cut to its last 400 characters; the implementation notes record that uncapped AIME
completions could exceed the deployment's per-minute token quota. Optimizer cost for the GPT-4.1 Mini AIME runs was about \$0.63 to \$0.68.
\paragraph{Finding.} Showing everything fits in one request here, but it is the main reason the design does not obviously scale to large
training sets, where sampling would be needed.

\subsection{Validation items as feedback, and its cost}
\label{app:d6}

cost is that $V$ is no longer held out. We quantify part of that cost with a simulation (Table~\ref{tab:inflation}). Take a validation set of $n$ items on which every

\subsection{Number of rounds}
\label{app:d7}
We use three rounds. Among the 11 runs with a unique best-validation round, the best was round 1 twice, round 2 six times, and round 3 three
times, so rounds 2 and 3 matter, but we did not run more than three rounds, and each additional round costs one optimizer call and two
solver passes.

\subsection{Settings we did not ablate}
\label{app:d8}
Optimizer temperature 0.7, the 95\% skip threshold, the validation fraction (0.5, and 0.35 for AIME and IFBench with GPT-4.1 Mini), the 400-character output cap, the
five-section schema (inherited from prior work, \S\ref{sec:related}), and seed 0 were fixed from the start or chosen by inspection. We did not tune them against test results to our knowledge, but we did not log a pre-registration, so this is a statement of
intent rather than a verifiable guarantee. Their effect is unmeasured.

\section{Mechanism History}
\label{app:history}
The final mechanism is the product of three earlier designs, each abandoned for a measured reason. An
early section-local design, in which an LLM judge attributed each failure to one prompt section before a scoped
edit, was retired because attribution was noisy and a strict regression gate rejected over 40\% of proposed edits
with no clear improvement trend. A whole-prompt design using exact-substring find/replace edits was retired after a mean
effect indistinguishable from zero across repeated seeds, a gate that rejected net-positive trades, and
round-to-round oscillation rather than compounding gains. An intermediate design that encouraged ``worked examples''
in the rewritten prompt caused the optimizer to paste training items nearly verbatim; test accuracy fell by 6.8 points
until an explicit anti-copying instruction was added, which the final optimizer prompt retains.

\section{Evaluation Details}
\label{app:eval}
Answer extraction and scoring are programmatic for every benchmark; the only model-scored quantity is PUPA's response quality.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{MMLU and Hearsay.} The solver ends with a line of the form \texttt{Answer: <letter>} (MMLU) or
\texttt{Answer: Yes/No} (Hearsay), which is extracted and compared with the gold label. The prompt may ask for visible
reasoning before that line.
\item \textbf{AIME.} The solver writes the final integer (0 to 999) on its own line as \texttt{\#\#\#\# <number>}; scoring is exact match.
\item \textbf{IFBench / IFEval.} Programmatic verifiers check each instruction in an item; an item counts as correct only if
every instruction is satisfied. Our verifier implements 32 of the 83 constraint types in the two benchmarks combined.
\item \textbf{PUPA.} A three-step pipeline. (1) The solver, using the optimized prompt, rewrites the private user query into a
redacted request. (2) A fixed external model (GPT-4.1) answers the redacted request. (3) The solver, using a fixed synthesis
instruction, combines the external answer with the original query into the final response. Leakage is the fraction of annotated
PII strings found in the redacted request (string matching, no model call). Quality is a GPT-5 judge's 0 to 1 score of the final
response against the dataset's reference response (judge completions are allowed 4096 tokens after the fix in
\S\ref{sec:bugs}). The composite is $(\text{quality}+(1-\text{leakage}))/2$; recovered/regressed use the threshold 0.7. Only the
step-1 prompt is optimized.
\item \textbf{Content filter.} Items whose call is refused by the provider's content filter are excluded from the denominator,
not scored as wrong: 2 of 40 PUPA test items and 3 of 66 MMLU professional-law test items, identically for both solvers.
\end{itemize}

\section{Optimizer Instructions and Seed Prompt}
\label{app:prompts}
\paragraph{Optimizer system prompt.} One template serves every benchmark; only the task description and the round budget $N$
change. In order, it tells the optimizer: (i) this is a measured multi-round experiment with at most $N$ rewrites; (ii) from round 2
it is shown the measured effect of its previous rewrite, in full, on both the mining and validation sets, and should first
diagnose which of its edits caused which recoveries and regressions, then keep what recovered items and revert or repair what
regressed them; (iii) the shipping rule, quoted below; (iv) a rule against turning shown items into examples, quoted below;
(v) that the solver has no hidden scratchpad, so reasoning helps only if written out, and that reasoning tasks differ from pure
recall tasks; (vi) the fixed five-section schema and the required final answer line; and (vii) that it may begin with an
\texttt{\#\# Analysis} section, which the parser discards.

\begin{quote}\small
Shipping rule: ``Every round commits unconditionally to a version history, and at the end, whichever COMMITTED round scores BEST
on validation accuracy is the one that actually ships [...] Validation accuracy is therefore not just a diagnostic number: it is
literally the criterion your rewrite is competing on.''
\end{quote}
\begin{quote}\small
Example rule: ``you are shown every failure, every success, and every validation item so you can DIAGNOSE what is going wrong,
NOT so you can turn them into illustrative examples.''
\end{quote}
The quoted shipping rule is from the current version. The nine runs that shipped the final round (\S\ref{sec:method}) used an
earlier version that instead said there is no best-round selection and that the last round ships; validation accuracy was reported
to the optimizer as a diagnostic only. In every reported run the optimizer saw flipped validation items in full.

\paragraph{Effect report.} From round 2 the user message contains: the previous text of every section the optimizer changed;
every mining item its rewrite recovered (question and gold answer); every mining item it regressed (question, the solver's new
wrong answer truncated to its last 400 characters, and the gold answer); the validation accuracy before and after; and the same
two lists for validation items. It ends with an instruction to diagnose causes on both sets before rewriting.

\section{Released Artifacts}

Each run directory contains: \texttt{config.json} (all settings); \texttt{metrics.json} (baseline and final test scores,
per-round recovered/regressed item identifiers on $M$ and counts on $V$, test-set recovered/regressed item identifiers, and
token and cost totals); \texttt{eval\_log.csv} (prediction, gold answer, and correctness for every evaluated item);

the validation/test, overlap, and trajectory tables from these files are included.
