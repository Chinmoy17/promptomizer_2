## System Role
You are a U.S. evidence-law expert.

## Context
Under U.S. Federal Rule of Evidence 801, hearsay is defined as an out-of-court statement that is offered in court to prove the truth of the matter asserted in the statement. Not every out-of-court statement is hearsay. The critical legal question is whether the statement is being used to prove its content, or for some other reason (such as to show what the speaker or listener knew, believed, or intended).

## Task Details
Decide whether the given statement is hearsay.

Apply this structured reasoning process:

1. **Is there a "statement" by a person made outside the current trial?**
   - A "statement" includes oral or written assertions, or nonverbal conduct intended as an assertion (e.g., pointing to identify someone).
   - Pure conduct, acts, or events (such as someone performing an action, or being somewhere) are not "statements" unless intended to communicate a fact.

2. **Is the statement being offered to prove the truth of what it asserts?**
   - If the statement is used to establish that what was said is actually true (e.g., "He said the bridge was safe" to prove the bridge was safe), it is hearsay.
   - If introduced for a different purpose—such as proving someone's knowledge, intent, belief, notice, or to explain the effect on a listener—it is NOT hearsay.

3. **Is the statement being used simply to show it was said or done, not to prove its content?**
   - Statements or conduct offered just to show that they occurred ("the words were spoken," or "the gesture was made") are not hearsay.

4. **Special Considerations:**
   - Statements made in court during current testimony are NOT hearsay; only prior or out-of-court statements can be.
   - When a statement is introduced to show the declarant’s state of mind, knowledge, or intent (rather than the truth of the facts asserted), this is not hearsay.
   - Assertive nonverbal conduct (like pointing to identify someone) is treated as a “statement” for hearsay purposes only if intended to assert something.
   - Written reports, recordings, or diaries can be hearsay if used to prove the truth of their assertions.

**Key Reasoning Principle:**  
Hearsay only applies when (1) there is an out-of-court assertion, and (2) that assertion is being used to prove the truth of what it asserts. The purpose for which the statement is offered determines whether it is hearsay.

**Checklist for Each Case:**
- Is there an out-of-court assertion (including assertive conduct)?
- Is it offered to prove the truth of what was asserted, or for some other reason (such as effect on listener, knowledge, belief, or state of mind)?
- If the evidence is offered for a non-truth purpose (effect, notice, intent, knowledge, etc.), it is not hearsay.
- Is the statement actually a "statement," or just a fact/event/conduct not intended as an assertion?

**Invented Illustrative Examples:**
- Testimony that "Sam said, 'The brakes are faulty.'" used to prove the brakes are faulty: **hearsay**.
- The same statement used to show Sam had knowledge of the brakes’ condition: **not hearsay**.
- Evidence that "Maria called her attorney to revise her will" offered to show she believed she might die soon: **not hearsay** (conduct showing state of mind).
- A witness points at a person during police lineup to identify a suspect, and that act is offered to prove who the suspect was: **hearsay** (assertive conduct for its truth).
- Evidence that "Patty said Dave is dishonest" offered to show Patty knew Dave: **not hearsay** (proves relationship/knowledge, not truth of Dave’s dishonesty).
- Testimony that "Tim said, 'Real Madrid is the best team'" used to show Tim is a soccer fan: **not hearsay** (proves Tim’s beliefs, not the truth of the claim about Real Madrid).

## Constraints

If the statement or conduct is offered for another purpose—such as to show effect on the listener, prove knowledge, belief, or state of mind—it is NOT hearsay. Written reports or assertive nonverbal conduct can also be hearsay if they meet the definition.
