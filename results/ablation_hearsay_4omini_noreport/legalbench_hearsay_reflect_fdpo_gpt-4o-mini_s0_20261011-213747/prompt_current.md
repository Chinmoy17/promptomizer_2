## Context
Under Federal Rule of Evidence 801(c), hearsay is an out-of-court statement that a party offers to prove the truth of the matter asserted. “Statement” means a person’s oral or written assertion, or nonverbal conduct intended as an assertion. Machines and animals are not declarants; but a human-authored report or record prepared outside court is a person’s statement. In-court testimony is not out-of-court.

Nonverbal conduct is a “statement” only if intended to communicate a fact (e.g., a deliberate identifying or accusatory gesture). Mere actions that are not intended as assertions are not statements. Questions and commands (including requests) are not assertions. Acts whose primary purpose is to do something rather than assert a fact (e.g., selecting, hiring, inviting, purchasing, or honoring someone) are generally non-assertive conduct.

## Task Details
Use this decision procedure and show brief reasoning before the final answer:

1) Identify the item: words, writing, or conduct.
   - Statement if: a person’s oral/written assertion, or nonverbal conduct intended to assert something (communicative gestures count).
   - Not a statement if: questions/commands/requests; or conduct not intended to communicate a fact (non-assertive acts such as selections, invitations, hires, purchases).
   - Human-authored documents/reports prepared outside court are statements by their authors; raw machine output (without human assertion) is not.

2) Was it made out of court? If it is testimony on the stand at this trial, it is not hearsay.

3) Purpose Fit Test: Separate the asserted proposition from the proponent’s use.
   - a) What proposition did the declarant assert (if any)?
   - b) What fact is the evidence offered to prove (as stated in the prompt)?
   - c) Apply the “if false, would it still help?” test:
     • If the evidence only helps if the asserted proposition is true, it is offered for its truth.
     • If it still has probative value even if the words are false, it is not offered for truth (non-hearsay use).

4) Truth-use quick check (apply only if it matches the stated purpose):
   - Effect on listener/notice: Non-truth use only when the person whose knowledge/notice is at issue is the listener and the probative force comes from the fact that they were told (probative even if the content was wrong). A speaker’s out-of-court claim “I heard/know X” used to prove that the speaker had notice/knowledge is offered for the truth of that claim and is hearsay.
   - Declarant’s state of mind/attitude/familiarity: Words used solely as circumstantial evidence of the speaker’s bias, support, hostility, or familiarity (without relying on the literal truth of any asserted fact) are non-hearsay.
   - Fact of utterance/time/aliveness: To prove only that words were spoken or that a communication occurred at a particular time (including that the speaker was alive then), irrespective of truth, is non-hearsay.
   - Verbal acts/legally operative words: where the law attaches significance to the fact the words were said.

5) Conclusion rule: If (a) out-of-court, (b) a “statement,” and (c) offered for its truth, then hearsay (Answer: Yes). Otherwise, not hearsay (Answer: No).
