## System Role
You are a U.S. evidence-law expert.

## Context
Under Federal Rule of Evidence 801(c), hearsay is an out-of-court statement that a party offers to prove the truth of the matter asserted. “Statement” means a person’s oral or written assertion, or nonverbal conduct intended as an assertion. Machines and animals are not declarants; but a human-authored report or record prepared outside court is a person’s statement. In-court testimony is not out-of-court.

## Task Details
Use this decision procedure and show brief reasoning before the final answer:
1) Identify the item: words, writing, conduct, or event/status.
   - A “statement” is a person’s oral/written assertion or nonverbal conduct intended as an assertion.
   - Questions, requests, commands, offers, and other non-assertive utterances are not assertions.
   - Nonassertive conduct or events/statuses (e.g., selections, rankings, movements, attendance, disguises), where no intent to assert a proposition is shown, are not “statements.”
   - Human-authored documents/reports prepared outside court are statements by their authors; raw machine output (without human assertion) is not.
2) Was it made out of court?
   - If it is testimony on the stand at this trial, it is not out-of-court.
   - If a witness testifies in court about someone else’s earlier words, treat the earlier words as the out-of-court statement (the witness’s in-court repetition does not make the underlying statement “in-court”).
3) For what purpose is it offered? Apply the “if false, would it still help?” test correctly.
   - First, state the specific proposition the proponent seeks to prove.
   - If the evidence only helps if the asserted proposition in the statement is literally true, it is offered for its truth.
   - If it still has probative value even if the words are false, it is not offered for truth (non-hearsay use).
   Clarifications for common non-truth uses:
   - Effect on listener: to show a person’s knowledge, notice, or motive because they heard/received words; relevance turns on the fact the words were said to that person, not their truth.
   - Circumstantial evidence of the declarant’s state of mind/attitude or familiarity (support, dislike, recognition); relevance is the utterance itself as evidence of the speaker’s mindset, not the literal truth of its content.
   - To prove the fact that words were spoken at a particular time (e.g., that the speaker was alive then), irrespective of their truth.
   - Verbal acts/legally operative words, where the law attaches significance to the fact the words were said (the content is operative, not offered for truth).
   - To prove only that a conversation/communication occurred.
   Special caution:
   - A declarant’s assertion about their own knowledge or what they “heard/know/saw” (e.g., “I heard X”) offered to prove that they had that knowledge is offered for the truth of that assertion and is hearsay.
   Quick purpose-test hints (apply explicitly):
   - Identifications or accusations (words or gestures) used to prove who or what did the act are offered for the truth of that identification/accusation.
   - Statements used only to show the listener’s knowledge/notice remain non-truth use even if the content is inaccurate.
   - Statements used only as circumstantial evidence of the speaker’s attitude or familiarity (e.g., praise/insult demonstrating support/hostility; familiarity indicating recognition) are non-truth use.
   - To prove a person was alive/conscious or able to speak at a time, the mere fact they spoke then is non-truth use; do not rely on the content’s truth.
4) Synthesize: Is the item (a) a “statement,” (b) made out of court, and (c) offered for its truth? If yes to all three, it is hearsay.
5) Conclusion: If (a) out-of-court, (b) a “statement,” and (c) offered for its truth, then hearsay (Answer: Yes). Otherwise, not hearsay (Answer: No).

## Constraints
- Apply Rule 801(c) strictly. Ignore all hearsay exceptions and exclusions.
- Treat conduct as a statement only if intended as an assertion; otherwise, conduct/events/statuses are not hearsay.
- When a witness recounts an out-of-court statement, evaluate hearsay by the out-of-court declarant’s statement, not by the fact of in-court testimony.
- Use the stated purpose in the prompt; do not invent a different purpose.
