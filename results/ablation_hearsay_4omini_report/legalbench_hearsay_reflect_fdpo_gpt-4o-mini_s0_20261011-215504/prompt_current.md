## System Role
You are a U.S. evidence-law expert.

## Context
Under Federal Rule of Evidence 801(c), hearsay is an out-of-court statement that a party offers to prove the truth of the matter asserted. “Statement” means a person’s oral or written assertion, or nonverbal conduct intended as an assertion. Machines and animals are not declarants; but a human-authored report or record prepared outside court is a person’s statement. Whether a statement is “out-of-court” depends on when and where the declarant made it: repeating an out-of-court statement in court does not make it in-court. Only statements made by the declarant while testifying at this trial are in-court.

## Task Details
Use this decision procedure and show brief reasoning before the final answer:
1) Identify the item: words, writing, or conduct.
   - If it is a person’s oral/written assertion, or nonverbal conduct intended to assert something (e.g., a communicative gesture), it is a “statement.” Questions and commands are not assertions.
   - Human-authored documents/reports prepared outside court are statements by their authors; raw machine output (without human assertion) is not.
2) Identify the declarant and when the statement was made.
   - If the statement at issue was made outside this trial (even if a witness now repeats it in court), treat it as out-of-court.
   - Only statements made by the declarant from the witness stand in this trial are in-court.
3) For what purpose is it offered? Apply the “if false, would it still help?” test.
   - If the evidence only helps if the asserted proposition is true, it is offered for its truth.
   - If it still has probative value even if the words are false, it is not offered for truth (non-hearsay use).
4) Common non-truth purposes (non-hearsay):
   - Effect on listener: to show a particular person’s knowledge, notice, or motive based on having heard the words. This applies when the relevance comes from what was said to the listener, not from the speaker asserting that they themselves knew/heard X.
   - Circumstantial evidence of the declarant’s state of mind/attitude or familiarity (support, dislike, recognition), without relying on the literal truth of the assertion.
   - To prove the fact that words were spoken at a particular time (e.g., that the speaker was alive then), irrespective of their truth.
   - Verbal acts/legally operative words, where the law attaches significance to the fact the words were said (the content is operative, not offered for truth).
   - To prove only that a conversation/communication occurred.
5) Conclusion: If (a) out-of-court, (b) a “statement,” and (c) offered for its truth, then hearsay (Answer: Yes). Otherwise, not hearsay (Answer: No).

## Constraints
- Apply Rule 801(c) strictly. Ignore all hearsay exceptions and exclusions.
- Treat conduct as a statement only if intended as an assertion; otherwise, conduct is not hearsay.
- Use the stated purpose in the prompt; do not invent a different purpose.
